#### Member: Olivia Asher, PhD

Currently based in NY

**How did you first become involved in science policy and SNAP?**

> *I got involved in science policy in January of 2025 after I realized that a career in research was not a viable option for me due to the national environment and my personal strengths and interests. I started out by going to Environmental Justice Day at the Georgia State Capitol with Science for Georgia. That year, I was the only Athens resident to attend. By the next year, I built up the science policy community in Athens and brought ten people with me to EJ day! I learned about SNAP in August 2025 from David Ramotowski. David and I both took the Science Diplomacy course from NSPN together and after the course ended he sent me a message asking if I would be interested in joining. Instantly, I was in and I got the Coalition of Athens Scientists, the local policy group I worked with in Georgia, to engage with SNAP as well.*

**Tell us about your research!**

> *For my PhD I studied fungi that live in 80% of plant roots and help plants get nutrients from the soil more easily! My research helped add to the available data on bacteria that associate with these important fungi.*

**What are you involved with in SNAP?**

> *I am involved with [Stance on Science](https://snapcoalition.org/initiatives/stance-on-science) in Georgia. I hosted and served on the organizing committee for the Spring 2026 Athens Candidate Forum on Energy and the Environment and am currently helping coordinate similar forums in Athens and Valdosta Georgia next month! I also try and jump in on tasks like doing the newsletter when I can. I love reading slack messages and sending messages so trust I will be in on that.*

#### Member: [Brandon Emanuel León](https://medium.com/@bel105)

University of Miami

**How did you first become involved in science policy?**

> *I got my start in science policy after attending a postdoc career panel. One of the speakers was a former AAAS Science & Technology Fellow and continues to work in science policy. He talked about the different skills involved, especially science communication with non-scientists which is something I am very passionate about. I was also drawn to the fact that science policy allows you to shape the impact of scientific discoveries beyond the lab and into society. While scientific and technological innovations come with many exciting benefits, it is also important to manage the costs and ensure those benefits are seen by all the relevant stakeholders. I am also from Northern Virginia (NOVA). I want to come back and live near my family and friends, and luckily many science policy jobs are located in the DMV. All of these factors lined up during that career panel and I knew that science policy was the direction I wanted to pursue after graduating.*

**Tell us about your research!**

> *The focus of my research is glioblastoma (GBM), a primary brain cancer that exhibits poor clinical outcomes. Patients survive an average of 12–15 months after diagnosis and the standard course of treatment hasn't changed in 21 years, so I am seeking to discover new therapeutic targets to improve patient survival.*
>
> *Specifically, I am studying the interactions between GBM cells and astrocytes in the vicinity of the tumor. Astrocytes are the most numerous type of brain cell and carry out several essential functions. They control what enters the brain, fuel neurons with energy, coordinate immune responses during infection or injury, and regulate levels of neurotransmitters. Interestingly, GBM cells reprogram astrocytes to increase their ability to grow and survive treatments like radiation and chemotherapy, but it is not entirely known how GBM cells do this.*
>
> *We know cancer cells release massive amounts of glutamate, a neurotransmitter, and that glutamate acts like a spark to fuel cancer cell growth. However, it isn't known whether GBM cell-derived glutamate reprograms astrocytes. Astrocytes have a number of glutamate transporters, receptors, and metabolic enzymes positioning them as a potential hub of glutamate dysregulation in glioblastoma. So far, I found that cancer cells that release higher levels of glutamate potently reprogram astrocytes. Additionally, astrocytes increase cancer cells growth when stimulated by glutamate or high glutamate-producing cancer cells. Currently, I am working on uncovering how glutamate reprograms astrocytes with these pro-tumorgenic functions to hopefully find a new treatment target to improve patient outcomes.*

**What are you involved with in SNAP?**

> *One recent initiative I am involved in is SNAP Visits. I had participated in a number of visits, but these were special. I brought along two students who were new to advocacy. We met and discussed their research and how their projects could address the issues facing Miami and Florida. Marla Marquez is a PhD candidate and our student government president and studies the shared mechanisms of synaptic degradation in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and traumatic brain disorder (TBI). Understanding and reversing this mechanism could yield new treatments for dementia for Florida's aging population. Gwen Pieklo is also a PhD candidate and studies how HIV and methamphetamine abuse changes the gut microbiome and negatively affects cognitive functions in patients. Miami exhibits one of the highest incidences of HIV infections in the country. The gut microbiome and the brain share a surprising connection and reversing the maladaptive changes induced by HIV and methamphetamine could improve deficits in cognition for patients. The NIH funds all of our research, and facing budget cuts, Marla, Gwen, and I framed NIH funding as a way to address issues that affect Miamians and Floridians.*

#### Member: [Emily Selland, PhD](https://medium.com/@eselland)

University of Notre Dame; based in Colorado

**How did you first become involved in SNAP?**

> *I joined SNAP during its establishment days, as I was a co-President of Notre Dame's Science Policy Initiative ([SPI](https://sites.nd.edu/spi-club/)) at the time, back in early 2025! It was a pretty perfect transition for me — I was loving what SPI had been able to achieve in the South Bend/Mishawaka region and Indiana more broadly in collaboration with other awesome Indiana graduate groups. But my time as co-President was almost done, and I was moving to a new state (Colorado). SNAP was the perfect opportunity for me to do and learn more, and to connect my Indiana science policy background to a nationwide perspective. I started my PhD not even knowing what science policy was, so I had a large learning curve through SPI. SNAP helped fill additional gaps both of knowledge and experience, allowing me to really grow with a supportive network of remarkable people (who are now wonderful friends!) from across the country. (I love SNAP, can you tell?!)*

**Tell us about your research!**

> *I study an infectious parasitic disease called [schistosomiasis](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/schistosomiasis). It is spread through snails in freshwater and is quite devastating, especially in rural and agricultural communities across the globe. In my research, I investigate how we can use environmentally based interventions to reduce the transmission of this parasite to people, protect ecosystems, and boost the sustainable development of communities. I take what's called a [Planetary Health](https://planetaryhealthalliance.org/what-is-planetary-health/) approach to disease control in the contexts I work in — we aren't just focused on the disease, but we think about the systemic drivers that perpetuate parasite transmission in these communities, including sustainability, agricultural systems, and socioeconomics! I use many different tools to do the work I do, including fieldwork, laboratory experiments, sociological surveys, and mathematical modeling.*
>
> *I love my research, half because it is solutions-oriented and half because I get to work with incredible people and diverse teams to make it happen. Some of the research into Rice-Fish Co-Culturing for parasite control, which [I led pilot studies on](https://news.nd.edu/news/rice-fish-farming-may-help-curb-schistosomiasis-while-increasing-food-production/), continuously adds more collaborators and research questions across disciplines, which has been so exciting to be a part of. The project has huge potential, which was recently highlighted in [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/12/nx-s1-5803170/farmers-fish-rice-snails-tropical-disease), [Climate Gendered](https://www.climategendered.com/native-fish-could-help-address-gendered-impacts-of-schistosomiasis-while-strengthening-local-food-systems-and-economies/), and [Agence France-Presse](https://today.rtl.lu/news/world/just-add-fish-scientists-pursue-more-rice-less-disease-in-senegal-paddies-2004337486) ([French version](https://www.france24.com/fr/info-en-continu/20260915-au-s%C3%A9n%C3%A9gal-l-ing%C3%A9nieuse-introduction-de-poissons-dans-des-rizi%C3%A8res-contre-la-bilharziose)), and I feel really proud to be part of a group pushing the envelope to take on grand challenges in global health from a system-wide lens.*

**What are you involved with in SNAP?**
